This week, we spent quite a bit of time on discussing the reasons why corporations back open source projects. I gained a perspective I wasn't used to - I've always viewed corporate involvement in open-source as largely predatory. I was always, of course, passively aware that much of open-source funding comes from these same corporations, but to me, it always felt like fattening a lamb before slaughter. Of course, that's not at all an accurate way of looking at it.

 <!--more-->

I'd always viewed corporate involvement as just paying to push passionate developers in directions that helped the company. However, the strategic reasons add a lot of nuance to this assumption. As it turns out, major reasons for corporate involvement involve avoiding vendor lock-in, providing an entry-point for talent, and creating shared tooling, especially for subcontractors.

This made a lot of sense for me when viewing through the lens of the ASWF, or the Academy Software Foundation. To see Adobe, AMD, Dreamworks, Microsoft, Skydance, some of the largest studios and tech companies in the world working together on building new technologies that _all_ of them can use, is a great example of how open source culture creates better products even for corporations involved. Looking back now, I think of the Adobe sRGB standard - a colorspace that's supported by every monitor and camera brand and improves accuracy for movie studios, who buy Apple and Dell and HP monitors, and use AMD hardware (which also has optimizations for this color space) to run Adobe software. It's a beautiful system where everyone contributing leads to everyone making money, and the consumer benefitting as well.

The other side of the question, however, is how open source developers _themselves_ can make money. And the idea of selling support, or selling install media, or selling the software as a service, all comes to mind. However, in the modern day, I feel that those offerings are rapidly dying out. Almost all open-source B2B SaaS options have been replaced by proprietary ones, because they have more money and can spend more on support and marketing. RHEL is still in use, sure, but they were acquired by IBM. Most SaaS offerings can be self-hosted, and with advancements in AI and in dev tooling, that moat is rapidly shrinking. Mozilla has started enabling ads and telemetry. I worry for the future of independent enterprise open-source, because right now, it looks bleak.
